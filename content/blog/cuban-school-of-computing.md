There is a particular flavour to the way Cuban computer scientists think about problems. It comes from an education system that treats mathematics not as a tool, but as the *lingua franca* of computing.

## The Foundation

At Universidad Central "Marta Abreu" de Las Villas (UCLV), the Computer Science curriculum begins with two years of intensive mathematics. Not "math for computer science" — mathematics. Real analysis, linear algebra, discrete mathematics, probability theory, and numerical methods are taught alongside — and often before — introductory programming.

This is not pedagogical nostalgia. It is a deliberate choice that shapes how students approach abstraction.

### Why This Matters

When you learn programming *after* you have internalized formal logic and mathematical proof, code is not syntax — it is a formal system. You don't ask "what does this function do?" You ask "what does this function *mean*?" The distinction is subtle but profound.

A programmer trained in this tradition:

- Reaches for invariants before implementation
- Thinks in terms of type correctness and algebraic structure
- Approaches debugging as a process of elimination grounded in formal reasoning
- Writes code that is *provably* correct, not just "it works on my machine"

## The Cost of Rigour

This approach has tradeoffs. Cuban graduates tend to be slower at producing code initially. They overthink simple problems. They reach for mathematical formalism when a simple loop would do.

But they also produce systems that *last*. The code written by engineers from this tradition tends to have fewer edge-case bugs, cleaner interfaces, and more maintainable architectures. The mathematical foundation acts as a cognitive safety net — catching errors at the design stage rather than in production.

## A Personal Reflection

I entered UCLV expecting to learn how to build software. Instead, I learned how to *think* about software. The mathematical training did not make me a better coder in the short term — it made me slower, more deliberate, more skeptical of my own assumptions.

The payoff came years later, when I found myself designing distributed systems and reaching for the same formal reasoning I had practiced on blackboards in Santa Clara. The tools were different, but the way of thought was identical.
